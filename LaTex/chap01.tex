\chapter{Pricing Mechanisms in Accommodation Services}
Pricing in accommodation services is a complex decision-making problem that involves working with multiple factors. This section describes mechanisms used for effective pricing strategies in the accommodation sector. We will discuss what mechanism are being used to approach dynamic pricing, what factors influence pricing decisions. The problem is formalized in terms of relevant parameters of a revenue management case. Finally, we will propose principles that will be developed into a formal model in subsequent chapter.

There are several key components to effective revenue management in accommodation services. Namely it is
\begin{itemize}
    \item Goals assessment
    \item Obtaining data
    \item Data and market analysis
    \item Forecasting
    \item Optimization process and pricing decisions
    \item Implementation
    \item Monitoring and control
\end{itemize}
\cite{Ivanov2014}. In this chapter we will discuss the processes behind first five components, which are essential for understanding the pricing mechanisms in accommodation services. With main focus of this thesis being \textit{forecasting} and \textit{and pricing decisions}. The last two components, \textit{implementation} and \textit{monitoring and control}, are important for correct execution of the pricing strategy at the operational level but as they are much more managerial in nature and depend heavily on concrete organization and human resources \cite{Petricek2021}, they will not be discussed in detail in this thesis.

A dynamic pricing model in proposed in subsequent chapter of this thesis, the approach to pricing predictions by this model is motivated by accessibility of real world sales data. Data that should be available to most operational managers in the accommodation industry, as maintaining sales records is standard practice and often even legal obligation. \cite{Ivanov2014} Furthermore, collecting demand-related data via web scraping has become more feasible than ever due to the widespread use of online booking platforms and AI tools. The purpose of this model is to simplify and help to set benchmarks for pricing decisions in operational processes of short term accommodation providers. So the aspects of revenue management will be discussed within this context of the later proposed model.

\section{Capacity Management}

Price management in accommodation services is not heavily researched topic \cite{AndersonXia2016}, many of the existing work bring very basic methods to the problem. Approaches are often borrowed from other industries, such as airline industry. These two industries are very similar in the sense that both are trying to maximize revenue through optimal allocation of finite resources to customers with stochastic behavior and willingness to pay \cite{Fiori2020}. However, the airline industry is in many ways specific and different from accommodation industry \cite{Bitran2003overview} let alone short-term non-hotel properties. This segment of the industry is often characterized by smaller providers with limited capacity and relative lack of flexibility in terms of service offerings. Therefore, not all methods and approaches are directly applicable to accommodation services. Accommodation industry has its own specific requirements and revenue management methods must be adapted to these needs.

\subsection{Segmentation}

Basis for effective capacity management is understanding of the accommodation market in terms of its segments. The definition of these segments is very specific to each particular accommodation provider and range of offered services. However, in general, the resulting market segments should fulfill following criteria \cite{Ivanov2014}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discrete
    \item Mutually exclusive
    \item Measurable
    \item Accessible
    \item Profitable
    \item Stable
\end{itemize}
Segmentation can be done based on various factors. The most basic segmentation factors include \cite{Petricek2021}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Purpose of stay (business or leisure)
    \item Group size (individual, families or group travelers)
    \item Booking time (early bookers vs. last minute bookers)
    \item Length of stay
\end{itemize}
These basic segmentation factors are particularly useful due to their compatibility with standard records maintained by accommodation providers. While not exhaustive, they can be further refined using additional variables such as demographics, distribution channels, customer behavior, and other relevant characteristics \cite{Ivanov2014}. Accurate identification of market segments is a key element of effective revenue management.

The traditional purpose of segmentation is mainly to develop profiles of potential customers. These profiles are compared to strengths and weaknesses of specific accommodation provider to choose the fitting customers and marketing streams \cite{Ivanov2014}. In the case of our model, the importance of segmentation is twofold. Beyond its marketing function, segmentation plays a critical role in data analysis. Correct identification of segmentation factors in sales data ensures that the units in managed portfolio are correctly matched to distinct market segments. This alignment is essential for using historical booking and pricing data to accurately use methods for demand forecasting and setting pricing for each segment.

Correct identification of customer segments is a key component of revenue management as a whole. Accurate segmentation helps to correctly select market segments that align with the provider's goals and service capabilities \cite{Petricek2021}. At the same time, it enables more meaningful data analysis allowing historical data to be interpreted within the appropriate segment context.

\subsection{Overbooking}
Overbooking is one of the traditional methods of capacity management famously heavily adopted by the airline industry. That is also why it is a relatively well-researched topic within revenue management problems. This method can be effective even in the hospitality industry and some models are built around optimizing allocation with overbooking in mind \cite{Pimentel2019}. However, short-term non-hotel accommodation services often face specific challenges that limit the applicability of overbooking strategies. These properties typically have limited capacity and a general lack of flexibility in accommodating guests. Furthermore, rules and constraints imposed by some online travel agencies (OTAs) and distribution channels do not allow for a simple implementation of overbooking. This makes it difficult to manage the risks associated with overbooking and use these strategies effectively unlike in larger hotel services \cite{Schwartz2025} or the airline industry.

\paragraph*{}
In the context of non-hotel accommodation services and the model presented in this thesis, traditional capacity management methods are often not directly applicable. Segmentation remains an essential data analysis tool, as precise segmentation of the managed portfolio enables more accurate demand forecasting and, consequently, better pricing decisions. The applicability of overbooking strategies is limited by the operational characteristics of this type of accommodation. Therefore, our data-driven approach focuses on realistic demand prediction as the foundation of the pricing strategy. The underlying structure of the model allows for the incorporation of overbooking strategies into the revenue management framework, ensuring that the model remains flexible and adaptable to various operational contexts within the accommodation industry.

\section{Price Mechanisms}
Pricing is a central component of revenue management. Deep understanding of pricing mechanisms is essential for informed decision-making in accommodation services. This thesis does not aim to address all aspects of pricing strategies, as these have been comprehensively covered in existing literature \cite{Ivanov2014, Petricek2021}. Instead, it focuses on key principles of dynamic pricing and their relationship to observable customer behavior patterns in this finite-resource environment, particularly in response to seasonality, trends, or events. The goal of this section is to establish foundation for the data-driven pricing model presented in subsequent chapters.

\subsection{Price elasticity of demand}
One of the fundamental concepts in pricing theory is the price elasticity of demand \cite{Ivanov2014}. This metric describes how sensitive the quantity demanded of a good or service is to changes in its price. In the context of accommodation services, elasticity models the relationship between price and the number of units sold, and it is directly linked to the total revenue generated by the provider.

Formally, price elasticity of demand $E_{pd}$ is defined as the change in quantity demanded divided by the change in price \cite{Petricek2021}:
\begin{equation}
    E_{pd} = \frac{\Delta Q / Q}{\Delta P / P} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \cdot \frac{P}{Q}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the quantity demanded, $P$ is the price. The value of price elasticity of demand (PED) provides insights into consumer behavior. Negative PED indicates that an increase in price leads to a decrease in demanded quantity, which is typical for most goods. The magnitude represents the degree of sensitivity of demand to price changes.

Estimating PED in practice requires historical data on both prices and quantities sold. A common approach is to fit a functional relationship between price and demand using regression analysis, where the logarithmic form is often preferred due to its ability to linearize the elasticity expression. Specifically, the constant elasticity demand function can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \log(Q) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(P) + \varepsilon
\end{equation}
In this formulation, the coefficient $\beta_1$ directly represents the price elasticity of demand \cite{Petricek2021}.

However, in raw historical sales data, price and demand often exhibit a positive correlation. This is primarily due to the finite-resource nature of accommodation services, where high-demand periods typically coincide with higher prices.
This phenomenon is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:price_demand_corr}, where long term real-world booking data show a positive correlation between price and demand, as price can and does increase during high-demand periods.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/price_demand_corr.png}
    \caption{Correlation between price and demand in real-world booking data}
    \label{fig:price_demand_corr}
\end{figure}

This positive correlation arises because both price and demand are influenced by external factors such as seasonality, market trends, and special events. For instance, during peak travel seasons or major events, both the willingness to pay and the actual demand for accommodation increase simultaneously. The situation can be also explained by customer segmentation analysis, as Leisure customers often have limited flexibility in their travel schedules, while business travelers tend to have lower price sensitivity in general \cite{Ivanov2014}.

Reliably estimating PED from historical selling data is challenging due to this endogeneity. More accurate estimation of PED from historical data could be achieved through using extensive analytical methods, advanced heuristics and precise segmentation or through different methods such as controlled experimentation \cite{Petricek2021}. However, this could be prohibitively difficult for many accommodation providers, as it would require extensive market data collection and analysis efforts in order to cover all possible segments and market conditions.

\paragraph*{}
Pricing mechanisms in accommodation services must account for the complex interplay between price, demand, and external factors such as seasonality and market trends. Price elasticity of demand is useful and common tool for understanding consumer behavior. However, estimation of price elasticity of demand from historical sales data is greatly complicated by the difficulty of correct data segmentation in order to keep external factors constant. While advanced methods could provide more accurate estimates, reliable estimation would likely be very resource intensive and challenging. Therefore, our proposed approach focuses on robust demand forecasting methods while utilizing the correlation between price and demand found in historical selling data instead of estimating price elasticity to form dynamic pricing decisions.

\section{Demand Prediction}
Demand prediction is crucial for effective pricing strategies in accommodation services. Understanding future demand patterns allows providers to optimize pricing decisions and maximize revenue. Various methods can be employed for demand forecasting, ranging from traditional statistical techniques to advanced machine learning algorithms \cite{Ampountolas2024}. In this section, we briefly outline some of the commonly used forecasting approaches and discuss their applicability.

Demand forecasting approaches can be generally divided into two main categories: purely regression-based methods and time series analysis methods \cite{Petricek2021}. As our proposed pricing model is data-driven and relies heavily on historical sales data, we will focus on these two categories of forecasting methods.


\subsection{Demand structure}
The goal of quantitative demand prediction is to accurately estimate future demand. Regarding methods utilizing time series analysis of historical data to predict future demand patterns, it is essential to understand the key components that occur in demand data \cite{Petricek2021}. These components include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trend}: The long-term movement in demand data, which can be upward, downward, or stable over time.
    \item \textbf{Seasonality}: Regular, often significant patterns that repeat over known periods (e.g., weekly or annual cycles). This distinguishes seasonality from a periodic component, where the period is fixed but unknown in advance.
    \item \textbf{Cyclicality}: Fluctuations in demand that occur over variable, often longer periods, making cyclicality considerably harder to model. These fluctuations are typically driven by broader economic or market forces; the economic cycle is a common example.
    \item \textbf{Irregular components}: Random variations in demand that cannot be attributed to trend, seasonality, or cyclicality. These are often caused by unforeseen or irregular events and anomalies.
\end{itemize}
Different forecasting techniques may be better suited to capture specific components, and a combination of methods may be necessary to achieve optimal results for a given environment.

Intention of trend modeling is to accurately capture the underlying direction of demand over time. This can be achieved using various techniques, such as moving averages, exponential smoothing, or polynomial regression. Trend modeling is particularly important for long-term demand forecasting. However, using methods like moving averages for trend modeling deletes seasonal patterns from the data, which can be detrimental for shorter-term forecasts \cite{Petricek2021}.

Seasonality modeling is particularly important for the aim of this thesis, as accommodation services often experience significant seasonal fluctuations in demand. "Seasonal" in this sense can mean both regular patterns occurring within selected periods (e.g., weekly or monthly) and irregular patterns caused by holidays and events. Capturing seasonality accurately is crucial for effective demand forecasting and pricing decisions.

\subsection{Forecasting methods}
One very basic method of Demand prediction that is relatively popular in practice is naive forecasting \cite{Petricek2021}. This method simply assumes that future demand will be equal some historical demand value. It could be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}_{t+h|t} = D_{t+h-m}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{D}_{t+h|t}$ is the forecasted demand for period $t+h$ made at time $t$, $D_{t+h-m}$ is the actual demand observed in the same period $h$ but $m$ periods ago (e.g., one year ago if $m$ corresponds to one year). While this method is easy to implement and understand, by itself it often fails to capture important patterns in the data, leading to suboptimal forecasts.

Moving averages represent another simple method for demand forecasting. This technique smooths out short-term fluctuations and highlights longer-term trends by averaging demand over a specified number of past periods. The moving average forecast can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}_{t+h|t} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} D_{t-i}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of periods used in the moving average. While this method can be effective for capturing trends, it may not adequately account for seasonality or irregular components in the data. Predictions made by moving averages reflect different patterns depending on the chosen window size $n$. For larger $n$, the predictions will be smoother and less responsive to recent changes, while smaller $n$ will make the forecasts more sensitive to recent fluctuations but potentially more volatile \cite{Petricek2021}.

Exponential smoothing methods are more advanced techniques extending the idea of moving averages approach by using all previous data points but giving exponentially decreasing weights to older observations. This approach allows for more responsive forecasts. Simple exponential smoothing
\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}_{t+1|t} = \alpha D_t + (1 - \alpha) \hat{D}_{t|t-1}
\end{equation}
sets the forecast $\hat{D}_{t+1|t}$ as a weighted average of the most recent actual demand $D_t$ and the previous forecast $\hat{D}_{t|t-1}$, with $0<\alpha<1$ being the smoothing parameter that determines the weight given to the most recent observation. These methods use weighted averages of historical data to create forecasts \cite{Ampountolas2024}. There are many concrete methods implementing this idea in some way \cite{Petricek2021}.

Another commonly used approach to demand forecasting is based on statistical regression. A particularly popular variant of linear regression combines a polynomial time trend with seasonal dummy variables to account for both long-term patterns and recurring seasonal effects \cite{Petricek2021}. This method is effective for modeling seasonality by fitting a trend component along with categorical indicators for each seasonal period:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{D}_{t|t-1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \gamma_i M_{i,t}
\end{equation}

Here, $M_{i,t}$ are dummy variables representing seasonal periods (e.g., months or days of the week), and one dummy is omitted to avoid multicollinearity.

This model structure allows the regression to capture non-linear growth or decline over time as well as recurring seasonal patterns. It is particularly useful in forecasting yearly or multi-year demand cycles where seasonality is regular and observable. This approach remains popular due to its simplicity and ease of interpretation but can lack in flexibility and the ability to capture complex seasonal structures \cite{Petricek2021}.

To address such temporal structures, time series models like SARIMA (Seasonal AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) are used. SARIMA extends the classic autocorrelation ARIMA model by including seasonal terms, making it suitable for forecasting demand that exhibits both trend and seasonal variation. SARIMA models account for autocorrelation, differencing to remove trends, and seasonal lags, and are usually denoted as:

\begin{equation}
    SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_s
\end{equation}

where $(p,d,q)$ are the non-seasonal orders (autoregression, differencing, and moving average), $(P,D,Q)$ are the seasonal counterparts, and $s$ is the seasonality period (e.g., $s = 7$ for weekly seasonality) \cite{KumarDubey2021}. SARIMA models can produce reliable forecasts when historical patterns are stable, but they typically require more parameter tuning and analysis.

The most modern approach to demand forecasting are machine learning algorithms. These methods can capture complex patterns in time series data and often outperform traditional statistical techniques, especially when dealing with large datasets with multiple influencing factors. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Random Forests are popular choices for demand prediction tasks. These algorithms can handle non-linear relationships and interactions between variables, making them suitable for complex demand forecasting scenarios. XGBoost, in particular, has gained popularity due to its ability to handle large datasets effectively \cite{Ampountolas2024}. These methods require careful data engineering and fine-tuning to achieve optimal performance but can provide significant improvements in forecast accuracy.

\paragraph*{}
Accurate demand prediction forms the core of the proposed pricing model. Its effectiveness depends primarily on the quality of demand forecasts, making the choice of an appropriate method particularly important. While the selected method can have significant impact on the overall performance of the pricing strategy, the underlying structure of the model can accommodate any forecasting technique that produces suitable output. This flexibility allows the model to adapt to various environments and forecasting capabilities of different accommodation providers as well as to future advancements in demand prediction methods.

\section{Price Setting}
In its most basic formulation, the problem of optimal pricing can be expressed as a revenue maximization task:

\begin{equation}
    \max_{P} R(P) = D(P) \cdot P
\end{equation}

where $D(P)$ is the demand as a function of price $P$, and $R(P)$ is the resulting revenue. This expression assumes full knowledge of the demand function including price demand elasticity, which is rarely available in real-world settings. Estimating the precise relationship between price and demand is often complicated by unobserved factors, data sparsity, and the influence of external variables.

While many theoretical models aim to solve this optimization problem numerically, there is limited empirical evidence showing that such approaches consistently outperform experienced human revenue managers in practice \cite{Rust2018OptimalDH}. Human experts often rely on intuition, context, and accumulated experience to make pricing decisions that are well-calibrated to the specific market and operational constraints.

\paragraph*{}
Approach of the model proposed in this thesis does not attempt to directly solve the optimal pricing problem. It focuses on forecasting demand accurately, and then deriving prices based on the observed relationship between demand and price in historical sales data. This implicitly incorporates the expertise of human decision-makers encoded in past pricing behavior. While this may not lead to analytically optimal prices in the strict mathematical sense, it provides a practical and scalable way to automate pricing decisions in line with historically effective strategies and with  potential for improvement through further analysis and refinement.
